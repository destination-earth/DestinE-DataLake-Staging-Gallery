FireIgnition: custom DEFAIR readers, lightning and new fires

A Destination Earth / DEFAIR use case: three new readers for LSA SAF and H SAF products, and a study of lightning ignition over the Iberian Peninsula from an AI ready cube

🚀 Launch in JupyterHub

Overview¶

What this notebook does¶

Every summer, some of the fires in the Iberian Peninsula are started by lightning. Two instruments on the same Meteosat Third Generation satellite now see both sides of that story: the Lightning Imager records every flash, and the Flexible Combined Imager detects the fires that appear afterwards. This notebook brings them together with three more pieces of the picture: how dry the vegetation was, how much rain fell with the storm, and how much water the air held before it. DEFAIR turns these six products from four different sources into one AI ready data cube. The cube is then used to answer two questions: is lightning followed by more new fires, and does dry weather make that effect stronger.

The notebook has two main sections:

  1. Readers. Three of the six products have no built-in reader in DEFAIR. The process of writing and register a reader is explained step by step in detail using the reader of the Fire Risk Map, to be used like a native built-in reader. The other two readers follow the same process.

  2. Analysis. The finished cube is opened from the project bucket with one call and used to answer two research questions.

The cube is built in the companion notebook Fire ignition cube preparation. It reads the raw products with the readers shown here, puts them on one regular grid, defines the case study settings and data preparation, defines the fire label and saves the result (cube) in the project’s bucket. You do not need to run it before this notebook!, it works only as support of the process and transparency.

Scientific background¶

In the Mediterranean Basin, lightning causes about 5 % of forest fires (Soler et al., 2021). Lightning fires often start in remote areas, where they can spread before anyone detects and suppresses them, and some become the largest fires of the summer. Dry thunderstorms, which bring lightning with little rain, are considered an important cause of these fires (Pérez-Invernón et al., 2021). In Catalonia, roughly one in 1,000 cloud-to-ground flashes starts a flaming wildfire (Soler et al., 2021). The question here is under which conditions a lightning strike leads to a fire.

This analysis builds on earlier studies of lightning ignition in the Iberian Peninsula. Vecín-Arias et al. (2016) combined lightning, vegetation, topography and weather data with logistic regression and random forests over central Spain. Rodrigues et al. (2023) used more than 17 million lightning strikes to train a machine learning model of the daily probability of lightning ignition across the peninsula, from fuel, vegetation and lightning characteristics. Pineda and Rigo (2017) examined the role of rain during thunderstorms. The delay between the strike and the detection of the fire is called the holdover time (window), and it is usually short: in the Swiss Alps, most fires were detected within 24 hours of the igniting strike (Moris et al., 2020). Moris et al. (2023) give a global overview of holdover times.

This use case applies the same approach with a new observing system. The MTG Lightning Imager is the first geostationary optical lightning detector over Europe (Enno et al., 2026). It observes all lightning, day and night, over the whole peninsula, and measures the duration and extent of each flash, which are tested here as possible predictors of ignition.

The Flexible Combined Imager on the same satellite detects fires every ten minutes, so lightning and fires are observed from one platform on the same time scale. Fire data from space are consistent across national borders and do not depend on national records of fire causes. Satellite products also describe the air before the storm and the rain during it, both important for the dry thunderstorms behind large fires (Pineda and Rigo, 2017; Pérez-Invernón et al., 2021).

All inputs are available operationally and come from EUMETSAT and its Satellite Application Facilities, so the same analysis can be repeated for each new fire season.

The study covers the 37 convective episodes between 1 July and 9 September 2026 over the Iberian Peninsula, on wildland cover used as mask for the aoi: needle-leaved trees, broadleaved deciduous and evergreen trees, shrubs and permanent herbaceous vegetation.

Research questions¶

RQ1. Do wildland cells hit by lightning have a higher probability of a new fire in the 48 hours after the first flash than the same cells without lightning under similar rain?

RQ2. Does that excess risk depend on how dry the fuel is and how much water the air held before the storm?

Hypotheses¶

RQ1 is answered using the relative risk (RR)
the probability of a new fire in cells with lightning divided by the probability in cells without it.
RR=P(fire∣lightning)P(fire∣no lightning)RR = \frac{P(\text{fire} \mid \text{lightning})} {P(\text{fire} \mid \text{no lightning})}RR=P(fire∣no lightning)P(fire∣lightning)​

  • H0, the null hypothesis: RR = 1. Cells with and without lightning have the same probability of a new fire.

    P(fire∣lightning)=P(fire∣no lightning)P(\text{fire} \mid \text{lightning}) = P(\text{fire} \mid \text{no lightning})P(fire∣lightning)=P(fire∣no lightning)

  • H1, the alternative hypothesis: RR > 1. A new fire is more likely where lightning struck.

H0 is rejected when the whole 95 % interval of the RR lies above 1.

RQ2 compares that relative risk between dry and wet conditions. If dry thunderstorms are the ones that start fires, the relative risk should be larger where the fuel is drier and where the air held less water: RR(dry) > RR(wet). RQ2 is exploratory: it measures the size and direction of the effect, does dryness change what a flash does?. Here a logistic regression and XGboost algorithms are used to answer the second question.

An episode, or storm day, is one calendar (July-September) day of the 2026 season (00:00 to 24:00 UTC) with significant lightning over the study area. A short storm that fell entirely between those slots can be missed. The season scan found 37 episodes.

Main assumptions¶

The companion notebook shows in detail how each of these is built.

  • One sample is one 10 km cell on one storm day. A cell is exposed when the Lightning Imager saw at least one flash in it that day. The instrument counts all lightning and cannot tell the flashes that reach the ground from those that stay in the cloud.

  • Each flash is moved back along the satellite’s line of sight to a cloud height of 10 km. Without this correction a flash over Iberia appears about one cell too far north (Bližňák and Sokol, 2026).

  • Each cell has its own 48 hour window. It starts at the first flash in the cell, or at the storm time of the day when no flash fell there. A fire first seen inside the window counts as new.

  • A cell is left out of a storm day when it, or one of its eight neighbours, already had a fire in the 48 hours before the window, or when the satellite could see fires in less than a quarter of its window.

  • Pixels with detections in three periods a week apart, or on twenty or more days, are treated as fixed heat sources such as gas flares, and removed together with their eight neighbours.

  • Each fire is counted once. A fire inside the windows of two storm days goes to the most recent storm.

  • A cell with lightning is compared with the same cell on other days without lightning and with similar rain, which keeps its terrain, vegetation and human activity fixed.(groups of cell with same conditions are compared)

  • The instability indices (k-index, lifted_index, prec_water_total) only exist under clear sky, so they are read between three hours and one hour before the storm time.

  • There is no record of what lit each fire, so the study measures association, not causation.

DEFAIR reader plugins¶

What is a reader?¶

DEFAIR does not open product files directly. It uses a reader: a small plugin that knows the structure of one file format and turns its contents into an xarray.Dataset.

A reader is a Python class that inherits from DataReaderPlugin and implements a single method, read(). The base class handles the underlying file access, including opening the raw file through self.open_file(path, source, source_kwargs), regardless of its storage location or source. The file may reside on a local disk, in an S3 bucket, or in the HDA. This abstraction means readers do not need to manage credentials, storage protocols, or file downloads. The same reader implementation can process files from any supported source without changes.

What does a reader enable?¶

Once a product has a reader, DEFAIR handles it like any other supported product. It can:

  • stream the data directly from S3,

  • read many files in one call,

  • crop, reproject and align it with other sources.

The product could also be converted outside DEFAIR and imported as plain arrays, but then none of these operations would be available. DEFAIR ships built-in readers for the METOP, MTG, Sentinel-3, MSG native and ERA5 families, among others. For any other product you can write your own reader and register it as a plugin, and DEFAIR then treats it exactly like a built-in one.

Building a reader step by step: the Fire Risk Map¶

This section writes the reader for the LSA SAF Fire Risk Map one step at a time. Each step adds one feature and tests the reader on the same file, the Fire Risk Map for the demo storm day. The file has five variables, the four codes of the Canadian Fire Weather Index system (FWI, FFMC, DMC, DC) and the danger class (Risk). After each step the notebook shows FWI again, so you can see what that step changed. The last step joins all the steps into one class, which is the lsa_frm reader in the readers package.

Step 1: open the file¶

The first version of the reader only opens the file and hands its contents to xarray. The class, FRMReader, inherits from DataReaderPlugin and has one method, read(), which receives the path of the file.

Inside read(), two lines do the work. self.open_file comes from DEFAIR and finds the file wherever it is stored, here in the project bucket. The second line opens it with xarray, with two options this format needs: phony_dims="sort" names the dimensions, because the HDF5 file has no names for them, and mask_and_scale=False keeps the values exactly as stored, so the raw content is visible.

load_reader turns the class into a working reader without installing or registering anything, which is the quickest way to test a reader while you write it. The last line shows the Fire Weather Index as read.

class FRMReader(DataReaderPlugin):
    """Fire Risk Map reader, step 1: open the file through DEFAIR's source layer."""

    def read(self, path, source=None, source_kwargs=None):
        f = self.open_file(path, source, source_kwargs)
        return xr.open_dataset(f, engine="h5netcdf", phony_dims="sort", mask_and_scale=False)

reader = load_reader("frm_step1", plugin_class=FRMReader, fresh=True)
raw = reader.read(frm_path(DEMO_EPISODE), **READ)
raw["FWI"]

The file is read, but it is not usable yet. The Fire Weather Index is stored as 16 bit integers, so a value of 2345 means 23.45, and -8000 marks pixels without data. The dimensions have no names (phony_dim_0, phony_dim_1) and no coordinates, so nothing says where each pixel is on Earth, and there is no time. A model fed with this would learn from numbers a hundred times too large and from a sea of -8000, and the field could not be combined with any other source. The next three steps fix each of these problems.

Step 2: physical values¶

The LSA SAF stores each variable as an integer X with its own attributes, and defines the physical value as X / SCALING_FACTOR + OFFSET, with MISSING_VALUE for pixels without data (LSA SAF, Product Output Format, SAF/LAND/IPMA/POF/1.2, in the LSA SAF documentation repository). These attribute names are not part of the CF conventions, the metadata standard that xarray, DEFAIR and most EO tools understand, so no tool applies them. The reader translates them into their CF equivalents, scale_factor = 1 / SCALING_FACTOR (CF multiplies where the LSA SAF divides), add_offset and _FillValue, and lets the standard decoder, xr.decode_cf, compute the physical values. The LSA SAF applies the same conversion when it publishes its own NetCDF files.

The five variables are loaded, the dimensions get their usual names, y for rows and x for columns, and each variable is told which grid mapping describes its projection, geostationary, which is added in step 4.

VARIABLES = ["FWI", "FFMC", "DMC", "DC", "Risk"]
ds = raw[VARIABLES].load()

ds = ds.rename({"phony_dim_0": "y", "phony_dim_1": "x"})
for v in VARIABLES:
    a = ds[v].attrs
    ds[v].attrs = {
        "scale_factor": np.float32(1 / a["SCALING_FACTOR"]),
        "add_offset": np.float32(a["OFFSET"]),
        "_FillValue": np.int16(a["MISSING_VALUE"]),
        "long_name": a["PRODUCT"],
        "units": "1",
        "grid_mapping": "geostationary",

ds = xr.decode_cf(ds)

Step 3: georeference¶

The pixels still have no position on Earth. The Fire Risk Map is not the full disc of the satellite but a window over Europe and North Africa. The file’s metadata says where that window sits, following the navigation standard of the Coordination Group for Meteorological Satellites (CGMS): the attributes COFF and LOFF are the column and the row of the window where the centre of the disc falls. The centre of the full disc is always the same pixel, so the row and column where the window starts follow by subtraction. Both numbers come from the header of the file.

With the start of the window and the size of a pixel, the centre of every column, x, and every row, y, is a simple sum. The projection is written in GEOS_ATTRS in CF form, so any tool can compute the latitude and longitude of a pixel from x and y.

Step 4: time and metadata¶

Every pixel now has a position, but the dataset does not say when it was observed. This step adds the date and completes the description of the dataset.

  • The date. The file stores it in the global attribute IMAGE_ACQUISITION_TIME. The reader turns it into a time dimension with one value, which is what later lets DEFAIR stack the files of different days.

  • The projection. In step 2 every variable was told that its projection is described by a variable called geostationary. That variable is created now as a coordinate that holds no data, only the attributes of the projection from step 3. It has to be a coordinate because that is where the reprojection tools of DEFAIR look for the projection of each variable.

  • The description. The global attributes record what the dataset is and where it comes from.

Written one after the other inside a single read() method, these four steps are the complete reader. It is stored as LSAFRMReaderPlugin in readers/lsa_frm.py, under the name lsa_frm, with three small additions that let DEFAIR drive it on its own:

  • _build_cdm declares what kind of data the reader returns, a grid on a geostationary projection. This tells DEFAIR that the source can be reprojected and resampled onto a target grid.

The reader returns its arrays lazily, so an archive larger than the memory of the machine can still be processed.

The other two readers¶

H SAF H61B: daily precipitation¶

The H61B reader follows the same four steps as the Fire Risk Map, written as one class. Most of its work is in two places. The files are gzipped NetCDF, so the reader decompresses them in memory before opening them. The date also needs care: the file sealed at 00:00 on day D+1 holds the rain of day D. The variable, acc_rr, is already in mm, so step 2 only renames the dimensions. H61B covers the full SEVIRI disc, the same disc as the Fire Risk Map, so the georeference reuses DISK_SIZE, DISK_EXTENT and GEOS_ATTRS from step 3.

The cell tests the class directly with load_reader. It then reads the same file by name through the registered reader hsaf_h61 and checks that both results are identical.

# H SAF H61B daily precipitation: the same four steps, written as one class.
# H61B covers the same SEVIRI full disc as the Fire Risk Map, so it reuses DISK_SIZE, DISK_EXTENT and GEOS_ATTRS from step 3.
class HSAFH61Reader(DataReaderPlugin):
    PRIORITY = 60
    FILENAME_PATTERN = re.compile(r"h61_(\d{8})_0000_24_fdk\.nc\.gz$")

    @classmethod
    def can_handle(cls, path):
        return bool(cls.FILENAME_PATTERN.search(str(path)))

    def read(self, path, source=None, source_kwargs=None):
        # 1. Open: the file is gzipped NetCDF, so it is decompressed in memory first
        sealed = pd.to_datetime(self.FILENAME_PATTERN.search(str(path)).group(1))
        with self.open_file(path, source, source_kwargs) as f:
            raw = gzip.decompress(f.read())

LSA SAF FRP-Pixel (LSA-509): fire detections¶

One LSA-509 file holds two products with different shapes, so the reader takes an extra argument, product, to choose which one to return:

  • "fires" is the list of fire detections, one row per fire. Each fire is placed at its parallax corrected position (LATITUDE_PARALLAX, LONGITUDE_PARALLAX), with its detection time ACQTIME, and gets an id that is unique across days. The reader declares these as point data with build_point_cdm, so DEFAIR knows not to resample them like a grid.

  • "quality" is a 1 km image on the MTG geostationary projection that shows where fire detection was possible. The reader converts the rows and columns of the FCI disc into projection coordinates in metres and adds the MTG projection. The MTG projection differs from the SEVIRI projection used by the other two readers.

RQ1: is lightning followed by more new fires?¶

The relative risk compares cells with and without lightning on the same storm day. It is the share of cells with lightning that had a new fire, divided by the share of cells without lightning that had one. A value of 1 means lightning makes no difference, and a value above 1 means cells hit by lightning burn more often.

A cell hit by lightning should be compared with a cell that is similar in every other way. The Mantel-Haenszel estimator (Mantel and Haenszel, 1959) does this by splitting the data into small groups, called strata. It computes the comparison inside each stratum and then combines them, giving more weight to the larger ones. Here each stratum is one cell under one rain class, so a cell with lightning is compared only with the same cell on days with similar rain and no lightning. A stratum counts only if it has days with lightning, days without lightning and at least one fire; the cell below reports how many do. For contrast, it also prints the crude relative risk, computed without strata.

Cells on the same day share the same storm and weather, so they are not independent of each other. The uncertainty is therefore estimated by resampling whole storm days. The 37 days are drawn at random with replacement, 1000 times, and the relative risk is recomputed each time. This is called a cluster bootstrap. The 95 % interval covers the middle 95 % of the 1000 values.

A fire can fall inside the windows of two storm days. The main rule assigns it to the storm day whose window started last. The last two lines of output repeat the estimate with two other rules. The first gives the fire to the last storm day with lightning in the cell, which can only raise the estimate. The second leaves out every fire shared by a day with lightning and a day without, which lowers it, because it also drops fires that lightning started on consecutive storm days. The main rule gives a value between the two.

def strata(df):
    """Fires and cells with and without lightning in every stratum of the study design."""
    return (df.assign(n1=df.exposed, n0=1 - df.exposed, a=df.fire_new * df.exposed, c=df.fire_new * (1 - df.exposed))
              .groupby(STRATA, observed=True)[["a", "c", "n1", "n0"]].sum())

def mantel_haenszel_rr(df):
    """Relative risk of the exposed cells, pooled over the strata of the study design."""
    s = strata(df)
    n = s.n1 + s.n0
    denominator = (s.c * s.n1 / n).sum()
    return (s.a * s.n0 / n).sum() / denominator if denominator > 0 else np.nan   # nan: no fire without lightning

def episode_bootstrap(df, stat, n):
    """The statistic recomputed on n resamples of whole episodes, drawn with replacement."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(SEED)
    parts = [g for _, g in df.groupby("episode")]
    return np.array([stat(pd.concat([parts[i] for i in rng.integers(0, len(parts), len(parts))])) for _ in range(n)])

def with_interval(df, stat, n):
    """The statistic and its 95 % interval from n resamples of whole episodes."""
    low, high = np.nanpercentile(episode_bootstrap(df, stat, n), [2.5, 97.5], axis=0)
    return stat(df), low, high

def repeated_fires(cube, sample, prefer_exposed=False, drop_mixed=False):
    """True where the fire of the cell is left out because it falls in the windows of several episodes.
    It is kept in the episode whose window started last; with prefer_exposed, in the last episode with
    lightning in the cell; with drop_mixed, a fire shared by episodes with and without lightning in the
    cell is left out of all of them. The cube was built with the first rule."""
    t = (xr.Dataset({"fire_first": cube.fire_first.where(sample), "start": cube.label_start, "exposed": cube.exposed})
         .to_dataframe().dropna(subset=["fire_first"]).reset_index())

    owner = t.loc[t.groupby(["y", "x", "fire_first"]).key.idxmax(), ["y", "x", "fire_first", "episode"]]
    dup = t.merge(owner.rename(columns={"episode": "owner"}), on=["y", "x", "fire_first"]).query("episode != owner")
    if drop_mixed:
        mixed = t.groupby(["y", "x", "fire_first"]).exposed.transform("nunique") > 1

         pd.Index(cube.y.values).get_indexer(dup.y),
         pd.Index(cube.x.values).get_indexer(dup.x)] = True
    return xr.DataArray(mask, coords=cube.fire_new.coords, dims=cube.fire_new.dims)

Two checks of the design¶

More lightning, more fires? If lightning starts fires, cells that get many flashes should burn more often than cells that get a few. The left panel shows the relative risk for four classes of flashes per cell, each compared with the cells without lightning. A relative risk that grows with the number of flashes is one of the strongest signs of a real effect that an observational study can give.

No lightning, no effect? A sound design finds nothing where there is nothing. The lightning maps are shuffled at random between storm days 200 times, so the flashes no longer fall where the fires are counted, and the relative risk is recomputed each time. The right panel shows those values, which should sit around 1. The share of shuffles that reach the real value is a permutation p value for RQ1.

Answer to RQ1¶

Probably yes, but one season is not enough to be sure. Cells hit by lightning had more new fires than the same cells on days with similar rain and no lightning, but the 95 % interval still includes 1, so H0 cannot be rejected.

The answer stays open because lightning lights only a small share of the fires, and the label counts new fires of every cause. In one season that leaves about a hundred fires in cells with lightning, and the effect of the flashes is mixed with many fires that would have happened anyway.

The checks point the same way, which makes the effect likely to be real. When the lightning is shuffled between days, the relative risk falls back to about 1 and rarely reaches the real value. Fires in cells with lightning appear sooner after the flash, which is what an ignition by lightning looks like. More flashes bring more fires, except in the cells with the most flashes, possibly because the strongest storms also bring the most rain; with so few fires that last class cannot be read.

How fires that fall inside the windows of two storm days are counted matters. Giving them to the last storm with lightning raises the estimate. Leaving out the ones shared by a day with and a day without lightning drops it below 1, but that rule removes about half of the fires in cells with lightning, because lightning often strikes the same area on consecutive days. The main rule, the most recent storm, can only err against lightning, so the estimate is, if anything, on the low side.

RQ2: does dry weather make lightning more dangerous?¶

Lightning is most likely to start a fire when it strikes without rain, in what is known as a dry thunderstorm (Rorig and Ferguson, 1999). The difference between a dry and a wet storm is decided before the storm begins, by how much water the air holds.

            DRY STORM                                WET STORM

   dry air before the storm               moist air before the storm
   (little precipitable water)            (much precipitable water)

        .------------------.                   .------------------.
       (    storm cloud     )                 (    storm cloud     )
        '------------------'                   '------------------'
            \     :  :  :                          \     | | | |
            /     rain dries up                    /     | | | |
            \     on the way down                  \     | | | |
   ~~~~~~~~~/~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~         ~~~~~~~~~/~~~~~|~|~|~|~~~~~~
      lightning hits dry vegetation          lightning hits wet vegetation
      nothing puts the spark out             the rain puts the spark out

      a new fire is MORE likely              a new fire is LESS likely

In the Mediterranean, the storms that start fires tend to have a high cloud base, less water in the air than usual and little rain (Pérez-Invernón et al., 2021). The GII measures that water in the air, and how unstable the air is, in the hours before the storm. Rorig and Ferguson (1999) told dry from wet storm days using the temperature difference between 850 and 500 hPa, the same difference the K-index is built on.

If this idea holds, cells hit by lightning should burn more often when the air held less water, when less rain fell and when the vegetation was drier. The rain itself is measured by H61B, but only as a daily total, which cannot say whether it fell before, with or after the lightning. The GII fills part of that gap by describing what kind of storm it was.

If dry storms are the ones that start fires, the excess risk of RQ1 should be larger in dry conditions than in wet ones. The cell below splits the sample into three equal parts (thirds) by the Fire Weather Index, which describes how dry the fuel is, and into thirds by the precipitable water before the storm, which describes how much water the air held. It then computes the relative risk of RQ1 inside each third. The ratio between the driest and the wettest third, with its bootstrap interval, says how much dry weather changes the effect. The precipitable water only exists under clear sky, so its thirds use the cells where it is available.

Can a model tell which flashes start a fire?¶

Two models look at the cells hit by lightning and try to tell the ones that burned from the ones that did not. The outcome is a new fire in the window, of any cause. Ten features describe each cell: the rain of the day; the three fuel moisture codes (frm_ffmc, frm_dmc, frm_dc), without the Fire Weather Index, which is built from them; the number of flashes, on a logarithmic scale, and their mean duration; the tree cover; the day of the season and the north to south position of the cell, which take care of seasonal and regional trends; and cell_base_rate, the new fire rate of the same cell on days without lightning, which carries the background risk of the place. The GII is left out of the models, because it only exists under clear sky and would remove many cells.

The main model is a logistic regression on standardised features, fitted without penalty so that its effects are not shrunk. XGBoost, a gradient boosted tree model that can capture non linear effects, is the contrast. Both are trained on the data as they are, without class weights, so their outputs can be read as probabilities. Each model is trained on 36 storm days and tested on the one left out, 37 times, so a day is never used for training and testing at once.

Two scores summarise the predictions. The AUC-PR measures how well a model ranks the cells that burned above the others; a model without skill scores the prevalence, the share of cells with a fire. The Brier score is the mean squared error of the predicted probabilities; lower is better. Accuracy is not used: with fewer than one cell in a hundred burning, a model that always says “no fire” is almost always right and has learned nothing.

Finally, the logistic regression is fitted on all the storm days to estimate its odds ratios, with 95 % intervals from the same cluster bootstrap. An odds ratio above 1 means the chance of a new fire rises with the feature, below 1 that it falls, and an interval that crosses 1 means the data cannot tell the direction. The three fuel codes describe the same system, so they share their effect and widen each other’s intervals.

Answer to RQ2¶

Not in a way this season can show. Split into dry and wet thirds, the sample keeps only a handful to a few dozen fires with lightning in each third. The relative risks of the thirds overlap, and the ratio between the driest and the wettest third stays close to 1 with very wide intervals. The dry thunderstorm idea is not contradicted; one season does not hold enough lightning fires to test it.

Neither model does clearly better than a model without skill, so the conditions of the day do not tell the cells that burned from the others. The only two features with a clear effect are the day of the season and the background fire rate of the cell. After lightning, a new fire is more likely late in the summer and in places that burn often anyway; the properties of the storm itself make no visible difference.

Limitations¶

The results hold within the limits of the data, gathered here in one place.

LimitationWhat it does to the results
Parallax corrected with one fixed heightFlashes from clouds much lower or higher than 10 km keep part of their shift, so a few land in a neighbouring cell. This pushes the relative risk towards 1
All lightning, not only ground strikesOnly flashes that reach the ground can light a fire, but the Lightning Imager also counts those inside the cloud. Exposure is broader than real contact with the ground
Fires of any causeThe label counts every new fire, whatever lit it. Lightning starts a small share of the fires, so its effect is measured on a small part of them
Daily satellite rainH61B is a daily total at about 3 km. It cannot tell whether the rain fell before or after a flash
Clear sky airThe instability indices only exist under clear sky, and they are read before the storm over the whole study area, not before the first flash in each cell
Fire detection limitThe FCI sees a fire only above a minimum power and not under clouds. Small or smouldering fires are missed, and the time to detection is an upper bound of the holdover
Fixed sources from the same seasonThe rules that remove industrial sources are computed on the season they clean, with reasoned thresholds, and removing eight neighbours can remove a real fire next to a source
Fuel is a forecastThe Fire Risk Map is a 24 hour forecast and carries the error of the forecast weather
Land cover of 2023Three years before the season studied
One seasonAll results come from 2026, which limits the number of fires and widens every interval

Conclusions¶

The notebook took six products from four providers to two answers. On the way it showed four things about working with DEFAIR.

  • Readers can be added. Three products without a reader came in through three small plugins, registered with a standard entry point and used exactly like the built-in readers.

  • Very different sources end on one grid. In the companion notebook, gridded and point products, geostationary and projected, become layers of the same cells through the same read, crop, reproject and align steps.

  • The cube is the handover. One open_zarr call is the whole link between the data preparation and the analysis, and the same cube feeds a stratified statistical comparison and two machine learning models.

  • A cube that carries its context supports careful statistics. Every cell has its label, window, identity, rain, visibility and storm day, so it can be compared with itself, the design can be checked against shuffled lightning, models can be tested without mixing days, cells the satellite could not see stay out of the sample, and each fire is counted once.

New fires were more frequent in cells hit by lightning than in the same cells without it, and the checks of the design support that this is not chance, but one season leaves the result short of statistical proof (RQ1). Dry fuel and dry air did not change the effect in a way the data can show, and the best signs of a fire after lightning were the time of the summer and the place (RQ2).

Lightning starts about 15 % of the forest fires of the Mediterranean Basin (Soler et al., 2021), and the label counts every new fire whatever its cause, so the effect of lightning is measured on a small part of the fires. More seasons, which the same pipeline can process, or records of fire causes would sharpen it. The results describe how often new fires followed lightning in one season, not the cause of any single fire.
